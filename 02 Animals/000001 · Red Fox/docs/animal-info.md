## Biological characteristics

### Physical characteristics
A slender dog with a long pointed snout, large upright pointed ears, a rusty-orange coat, white chin, throat and chest, black 'stockings' on the legs, black backs to the ears and a long bushy tail usually ending in a white tip [1].

### Size and weight
Head and body 455–900 mm, tail 300–555 mm, weight 3–14 kg; males are slightly larger than females [1].

### Lifespan
About 3 years on average in the wild (individual records are longer); 10–12 years in captivity [1].

### Diet and feeding behaviour
An opportunistic omnivore: mainly rodents and rabbits, also insects, fruit and carrion; it eats about 0.5–1 kg of food a day [1].

### Habitat
Forest, tundra, prairie, desert, mountains, farmland and towns, from sea level to 4,500 m [1].

### Geographic distribution and native regions
Much of the Northern Hemisphere, from the Arctic Circle to Central America, the central Asian steppes and North Africa; introduced to Australia and the Falkland Islands. It has the widest distribution of any canid [1].

### Habits and behaviour
Mostly solitary and at least partly territorial; it hunts small mammals by standing still, listening, then leaping and pinning the prey to the ground (the 'mousing' pounce) [1].

### Social structure
A home range usually holds one adult male and one or two adult females with their young; some groups include non-breeding helpers [1].

### Reproduction
Gestation typically 51–53 days; litters average about 5 pups (range 1–13) [1].

### Predators and threats
Eagles, coyotes, grey wolves, bears and mountain lions; humans (hunting, trapping, fur farming) are the most significant [1].

## Additional information

### Unique adaptations
It finds hidden rodents by standing still and listening before it pounces [1]; mousing leaps aimed roughly north-east (NNE) succeed far more often than leaps in other directions, which the authors explain by a possible use of the Earth's magnetic field [3].

### Ecological role and importance
A predator of rodents and rabbits, and prey of larger carnivores and raptors [1]; where introduced (Australia) it is a major threat to native wildlife (general knowledge, not from the sources above).

### Interesting facts
- Foxes pounce on mice under snow, and leaps aimed roughly north-east land far more often. A magnetic sense, maybe! [3]
- It has the widest range of any wild dog, from the Arctic Circle to North Africa. [1]

### Uncertain or variable information
Average wild lifespan figures vary between sources (3 to 12 years in the ADW tables) [1]; the magnetic-alignment explanation of the pounce is a hypothesis supported by field observations, not a proven mechanism [3].
