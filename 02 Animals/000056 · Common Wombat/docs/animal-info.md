## Biological characteristics

### Physical characteristics
Barrel-shaped, short-legged marsupial with coarse brown-grey fur, broad head, small rounded ears, large bare black nose, and powerful flattened digging claws. [1]

### Size and weight
Length 0.85–1.15 m; shoulder height ~35 cm; weight 22–39 kg. [1]

### Lifespan
15–20 years in the wild (over 25 in captivity). [1]

### Diet and feeding behaviour
Herbivorous grazer of fibrous grasses, sedges, roots, and moss, aided by ever-growing rootless molars. [1]

### Habitat
Temperate wet and dry sclerophyll forests, heathlands, and alpine regions of southeastern Australia and Tasmania. [1]

### Geographic distribution and native regions
Southeastern Australia (New South Wales, Victoria, South Australia) and Tasmania. [1]

### Habits and behaviour
Nocturnal and fossorial, excavating extensive burrow systems up to 20 m long; surprisingly fast sprinter (40 km/h) over short distances. [1]

### Social structure
Mostly solitary, defending home ranges around core burrows. [1]

### Reproduction
Gestation ~20–22 days; single joey stays in a backward-facing pouch (so dirt doesn't enter while digging) for 6–10 months. [1]

### Predators and threats
Sarcoptic mange, road mortality, and habitat clearing. [1, 2]

## Additional information

### Unique adaptations
Backward-facing pouch protects young during excavation; cartilage-armoured rump defends burrow entrances. [1]

### Ecological role and importance
Major soil aerator whose abandoned burrows provide crucial shelter for wallabies, echidnas, and reptiles during bushfires. [1]

### Interesting facts
- The only known animal in the world that produces cube-shaped droppings—so they don't roll away when marking territory! [1]
- Its rear end is reinforced with thick cartilage plates; when threatened, it blocks its burrow with its armoured rump. [1]

### Uncertain or variable information
Three subspecies exist: mainland (V. u. hirsutus), Tasmanian (V. u. tasmaniensis), and Flinders Island (V. u. ursinus).
