## Biological characteristics

### Physical characteristics
Largest of the four lynx species, with long legs, oversized furred paws, a flared facial ruff, long black ear tufts and a short black-tipped bobtail [1].

### Size and weight
Head-and-body length 80–130 cm, tail 11–24.5 cm, shoulder height 60–75 cm, and weight 18–30 kg (males up to 38 kg) [1].

### Lifespan
14–17 years in the wild and up to 24 years in captivity [1].

### Diet and feeding behaviour
Specializes in small-to-medium ungulates such as roe deer, chamois, musk deer and reindeer, supplemented by hares, grouse and foxes [1].

### Habitat
Boreal and temperate forests with dense undergrowth, as well as forested mountain slopes and scrublands [1, 2].

### Geographic distribution and native regions
Across Europe, Russia, Siberia, Central Asia and the Tibetan Plateau [2].

### Habits and behaviour
Solitary ambush hunter active mainly at dawn and dusk, capable of silent movement through deep snow [1].

### Social structure
Strictly solitary with large overlapping male-female home ranges [1].

### Reproduction
Gestation 67–74 days; 1–4 kittens born in May–June [1].

### Predators and threats
Locally threatened by habitat fragmentation, illegal poaching and road mortality, though globally stable [2, 3].

## Additional information

### Unique adaptations
Large winter foot pads double surface area on snow, and black ear tufts enhance acoustic localization [1].

### Ecological role and importance
Key predator of European and Asian forest ungulates, preventing overbrowsing of woodland regeneration [1, 3].

### Interesting facts
- Its long black ear tufts act like sensitive antennae, channeling faint forest sounds into its ears. [1]
- Oversized winter paws grow thick fur between the toes, spreading its weight like built-in snowshoes. [1]

### Uncertain or variable information
Total length of 1.3 m reflects body plus short bobbed tail [1].
