## Biological characteristics

### Physical characteristics
A large bear with a long neck and narrow head, small ears, black skin and translucent, unpigmented guard hairs that look white; broad forepaws [1].

### Size and weight
Males up to 2.5 m long, females 1.8–2 m; up to 1.6 m at the shoulder; males 300–800 kg, females 150–300 kg [1].

### Lifespan
25–30 years in the wild; about 38 on average in captivity (ADW table) [1].

### Diet and feeding behaviour
Mainly ringed seals, also bearded, harp and hooded seals and walruses; it still-hunts at seals' breathing holes in the ice; it does not cache uneaten meat [1].

### Habitat
Arctic pack ice, especially the ice edge and pressure ridges [1].

### Geographic distribution and native regions
Circumpolar Arctic; wanderers have reached Iceland, southern Greenland and even Maine [1].

### Habits and behaviour
An excellent swimmer, paddling with its broad forepaws [1].

### Social structure
Mostly solitary apart from mothers with cubs and mating pairs (general knowledge; not covered by the sources above, to verify).

### Reproduction
Pregnant females dig snow dens near the coast in October–November; litters of 1–4 (about 2) cubs weighing about 600 g at birth and 10–15 kg when they leave the den [1].

### Predators and threats
Adults have no natural predators; sea-ice loss from climate change is the main threat [1][2].

## Additional information

### Unique adaptations
Clear hair over black skin, thick fat, broad paddling forepaws [1].

### Ecological role and importance
The apex predator of the Arctic sea-ice ecosystem [1].

### Interesting facts
- Its fur isn't actually white! The hairs are clear, and the skin underneath is black. [1]
- Huge front paws work like paddles, making it an excellent swimmer. [1]

### Uncertain or variable information
Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List (2015); the global population trend is listed as unknown because subpopulations differ [2]. Captive lifespan figures in the ADW table look high [1].
