## Biological characteristics

### Physical characteristics
Largest member of the viverrid family, with coarse black fur tipped with silver-grey, white-bordered tufted ears, long white whiskers and a thick muscular prehensile tail [1].

### Size and weight
Head-and-body length 61–96 cm, tail 56–89 cm, and weight 9–20 kg (females average ~20% larger than males) [1].

### Lifespan
15–20 years in the wild and over 25 years in captivity [1].

### Diet and feeding behaviour
Primarily frugivorous—relying heavily on strangler figs—while also eating shoots, leaves, insects, birds and small vertebrates [1].

### Habitat
Tall tropical evergreen and deciduous forests across South and Southeast Asia [1, 2].

### Geographic distribution and native regions
Bangladesh, Bhutan, Cambodia, China, India, Indonesia (Java, Kalimantan, Sumatra), Laos, Malaysia, Myanmar, Nepal, Philippines (Palawan), Thailand and Vietnam [2].

### Habits and behaviour
Arboreal and crepuscular/nocturnal, moving deliberately through the canopy using its prehensile tail as a fifth limb [1].

### Social structure
Mostly solitary or in small mother-offspring groups [1].

### Reproduction
Gestation 90–92 days (exhibits embryonic diapause); typically 1–3 cubs [1].

### Predators and threats
Rapid lowland rainforest loss, hunting for meat and traditional medicine, and capture for the pet trade [2].

## Additional information

### Unique adaptations
Secretes 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline (the aroma compound of popped popcorn) from subcaudal scent glands to mark branches [3].

### Ecological role and importance
Vital seed disperser for strangler figs whose seeds germinate faster after passing through a binturong's gut [1].

### Interesting facts
- Scent glands under its tail release a compound (2-AP) that smells remarkably like warm buttered popcorn. [3]
- It is one of only two carnivores—alongside the kinkajou—with a truly prehensile tail strong enough to support its full weight. [1]

### Uncertain or variable information
Total nose-to-tail length of 1.65 m combines ADW body and tail averages [1].
