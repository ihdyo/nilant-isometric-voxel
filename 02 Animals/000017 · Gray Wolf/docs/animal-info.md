## Biological characteristics

### Physical characteristics
Coat from pure white (Arctic) to grizzled white, black, grey, cinnamon and brown, or near black; dense underfur [1].

### Size and weight
Head and body 0.87–1.3 m, tail 0.35–0.52 m, 60–90 cm at the shoulder; males 30–80 kg, females 23–55 kg [1].

### Lifespan
Up to 13 years in the wild (about 5 on average), up to 15 in captivity [1].

### Diet and feeding behaviour
Large prey such as moose, elk, bison, musk oxen and reindeer, usually hunted in packs; lone wolves take beavers and rabbits; up to 9 kg of meat in one meal [1].

### Habitat
From Arctic tundra to forest, prairie and arid land [1].

### Geographic distribution and native regions
Once most of the Northern Hemisphere; today parts of the United States, Alaska, Canada, Eurasia and a small population in Mexico [1].

### Habits and behaviour
Travels up to 200 km a day, mostly at night; runs at 55–70 km/h; howls to gather the pack and mark its territory [1].

### Social structure
Packs of 2 to 36, mostly 5 to 9: a breeding pair and their offspring [1].

### Reproduction
Gestation 60–63 days; 1–14 pups, about 6–7 on average, born blind and deaf [1].

### Predators and threats
Habitat loss and persecution by people [1].

## Additional information

### Unique adaptations
Pack hunting brings down prey far larger than one wolf [1].

### Ecological role and importance
A top predator of large hoofed animals [1].

### Interesting facts
- Every pet dog on Earth, from Chihuahua to Great Dane, descends from the gray wolf. [1]
- Howls help a pack find each other and tell other packs: this patch is taken! [1]

### Uncertain or variable information
ADW's lifespan tables disagree (averages of 5, 10 and 16 years in the wild) [1].
