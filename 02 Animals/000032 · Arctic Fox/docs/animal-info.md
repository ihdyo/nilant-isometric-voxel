## Biological characteristics

### Physical characteristics
Compact fox with short legs, rounded ears, a short muzzle, furred paw soles and a thick multi-layered coat that turns pure white in winter [1].

### Size and weight
Head-and-body length 46–68 cm, tail 25–35 cm, shoulder height 25–30 cm, and weight 3–8 kg [1].

### Lifespan
3–6 years in the wild (longer in captivity) [1].

### Diet and feeding behaviour
Opportunistic predator and scavenger feeding on lemmings, voles, seabirds, eggs, berries, fish and polar bear carrion [1].

### Habitat
Circumpolar Arctic tundra, coastal areas and winter sea ice [1, 2].

### Geographic distribution and native regions
Circumpolar across northern Canada, Alaska, Greenland, Iceland, Svalbard, Scandinavia and northern Russia [2].

### Habits and behaviour
Active year-round without hibernating; uses keen hearing to plunge headfirst into snowdrifts after subnivean lemmings [1].

### Social structure
Monogamous breeding pairs occupy multi-generational earthen dens during summer [1].

### Reproduction
Gestation about 52 days; large litters of 5–10 (up to 19) pups match lemming boom years [1].

### Predators and threats
Global population stable, though red fox northward expansion and sea-ice loss threaten southern populations [2].

## Additional information

### Unique adaptations
Lowest lower critical temperature of any mammal (−40 to −70 °C), aided by countercurrent heat exchange in the paws [3].

### Ecological role and importance
Primary terrestrial predator of Arctic tundra rodents and nutrient concentrator around den sites [1].

### Interesting facts
- Its coat changes color with the seasons—pure snow-white in winter and charcoal-brown in summer. [1]
- It can endure temperatures down to −70 °C without shivering thanks to the warmest fur of any mammal. [3]

### Uncertain or variable information
Total length of 0.85 m reflects body plus bushy tail [1].
